## Coalitions weaken the bound

An attacker who controls a member pool can skew its reserves to drag the aggregate toward the quote
they want.

| | Enforced quote |
| --- | --- |
| Before coalition skew | 6.3159 |
| After | 9.2989 |
| **Loosened by** | **4,722 bps, roughly half** |

## What the bound keys off, and what the data says about it

Knot compares two quotes and takes the worse one. It never reads how far apart they are. That
distinction is not stylistic: the two forms of the claim were measured separately against real
chain data and they do not agree.

| Form of the claim | Result | Does Knot use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Divergence **magnitude** predicts toxicity | **False.** Spearman rho = -0.130, the wrong direction. Placebo 0.0005, so it is signal and not noise. Holds across three markout horizons and across pair families | **No.** Magnitude is never an input |
| Divergence **direction** predicts toxicity | **True.** Locally-favourable swaps average **+0.331 bps** markout, which is harmful. The rest average **-0.991 bps** | **Yes.** This is the only condition the bound engages on |

Measured across 67,743 swaps in every multi-pool pair family on Base with enough volume.

The bound engages on exactly one condition: the local pool would pay the taker more than the
pair's combined reserves support. That condition is what "locally favourable" means, and it is
the population the data marks harmful. A pool quoting worse than its pair is left alone, however
far from the aggregate it sits.

`test/DirectionalSelectivity.t.sol` proves the selectivity mechanically, on both the exact-input
and exact-output branches, and pins down that magnitude is never consulted.

<Warning>
This supports the choice of signal. It does not measure the benefit on a live federation. The
markout figures come from an offline harness against sibling pools at different fee tiers on
Base, not from a Knot deployment, and only four multi-tier pair families in that set carried
enough volume. Treat it as directional evidence, which is how it was recorded.
</Warning>

Two things bound the damage, neither of which eliminates it: membership is permissioned, so an
attacker must first be admitted; and skewing the aggregate costs real capital proportional to the
influence sought.

**Treat permissioned membership as load-bearing security, not administrative convenience.**

## Structural constraints

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="New pools only">
    A pool's hook address is fixed at creation, so existing pools cannot join a federation. Knot
    applies to pools deployed into one from the start.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Non-member pools stay exploitable">
    Anyone can permissionlessly deploy a pool outside the federation for the same pair. Knot
    protects its members, not the pair universally.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="The aggregate is not an oracle">
    It is an internal reference derived from member reserves. It is not a fair-price feed and
    should not be consumed as one.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Non-standard tokens">
    Fee-on-transfer and rebasing tokens need separate deployment testing.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## What the clamp actually taxes

Across 1,001 randomised configurations the bound **never bound on a divergence-widening trade**.
Every trade it bites is one that was realigning the pool toward the pair's consensus, an
arbitrageur, taking profit funded by LPs.

Retaining that value for LPs is the mechanism working as designed. The honest framing is that Knot
is a fee on cross-pool arbitrage, redistributed to LPs, rather than a filter that blocks attackers.
